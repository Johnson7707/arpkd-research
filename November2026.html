Clarifications to Issue 3 (October 2026) CLARIFICATION · Oct 5, 2026

Base editing. Issue 3 described our daughter's variants as confirmed adenine base editor substrates. Both are transition mutations that adenine base editing can in principle correct, but guide design, editing window and PAM context have not been tested at either site, so "predicted" is the accurate word.

Gene insertion (HITI). Issue 3 presented the Ginn et al. approach as mutation-agnostic. The donor has to carry all coding sequence downstream of the insertion point. That is small for OTC but far beyond single-AAV capacity for PKHD1 (12.2 kb coding sequence), so the approach cannot be assumed to transfer. The authors also reported that many integration events were in more complex arrangements than predicted.

AAV safety. Issue 3 stated, without a citation, that liver-directed AAV has caused deaths in children with pre-existing cholangiopathy. The published record we have reviewed since reports fatal liver failure after high-dose AAV8 in X-linked myotubular myopathy (see the Kim et al. item above), A 2020 editorial on that trial (Wilson and Flotte) reports two deaths from liver toxicity at three times the earlier dose but does not mention pre-existing liver disease, and the 2026 meta-analysis's attribution of a cholestasis finding to it could not be confirmed. We are reading the primary trial report and will restate the claim only with citations.

🧬 Molecular Biology & Biliary Cascade

🧬 MOLECULAR BIOLOGY & BILIARY CASCADE

Noted (Tier 3 lead): CFTR assembles with the Hippo proteins TAZ and YAP in ADPKD mouse kidney; the CFTR corrector VX-809 reverses the pattern — ADPKD data, no ARPKD or biliary data yet 📌 NOTED · Oct 1, 2026

Sharma A, Afshani M, Ciobanu C, Sharma T, Kress JT, Guggino WB, Cebotaru L. "The ion channel, CFTR, assembles with HIPPO pathway proteins TAZ and YAP in polycystic kidney disease." J Biol Chem 302(7):113152, July 2026. DOI: 10.1016/j.jbc.2026.113152. Open access (CC BY-NC-ND). ✅ Full text read October 1, 2026.

In Pkd1 R3277C mice and Pkd1-null kidney cells, CFTR co-immunoprecipitates with TAZ and YAP. In cystic epithelium all three accumulate at the apical membrane and nuclear TAZ/YAP is higher; VX-809 (lumacaftor) moves them back toward the normal basolateral pattern and lowers nuclear TAZ/YAP. Why it is noted here: the same group's ARPKD cholangiocyte study (Sharma, Cebotaru et al., Cell Calcium, in press) showed CFTR correctors reducing cholangiocyte proliferation and cyst growth. Whether the CFTR–TAZ/YAP link exists in Pkhd1-deficient cholangiocytes has not been tested. ⚠️ Limits: ADPKD mouse model, kidney only, correlative binding and imaging data; the authors' causal wording goes further than the data.

Noted (Tier 3, resource): open single-cell atlas of the healthy pediatric liver — children's Kupffer-like cells are more immune-active than adults'; cholangiocyte results not reported 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

Edgar RD, Nakib D, Camat D, et al. Hepatol Commun 9(11):e0813, 2025. DOI: 10.1097/HC9.0000000000000813 (open access; full text read). 42,660 cells from 9 healthy pediatric donors (ages 1–19) versus 26,372 adult cells, plus intestinal-failure liver disease biopsies. Pediatric Kupffer-like cells express more CCL3, CCL4 and IL1B than adult ones (IL-1β secretion confirmed). Used as a comparator, the atlas also showed higher MHC I/II signaling in cholangiocytes from biliary atresia and choledochal cysts than in healthy pediatric cells, and one spatial sample captured a bile duct hamartoma (data to be released). Why it is noted: adult-derived atlases lack a pediatric baseline. ⚠️ Limits: healthy donors only; cholangiocyte numbers and PKHD1 detection are not reported. Data are open (CELLxGENE, Human Cell Atlas portal).

Noted (Tier 4, adjacent): a sympathetic nerve signal, NPY, drives pro-fibrotic macrophage states in two mouse liver fibrosis models, one of them cholestatic 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

Lin Q, Zheng X, et al. Cell Mol Life Sci 83(1):346, 2026 (DOI 10.1007/s00018-026-06428-2, as given in a news summary; the paper itself was not read). NPY rose in human and mouse fibrotic livers and acted through macrophage NPY1R/NPY2R and PI3K–PAK1; mice lacking NPY, or with the receptors silenced, had less fibrosis in carbon tetrachloride and DDC (cholestatic) models. The authors say a direct effect on stellate cells is not validated. Why it is noted: adds a nerve-derived signal to the macrophage-driven fibrosis thread. ⚠️ Limits: mouse chemical-injury models; untested in ARPKD; no stress or diet inference follows.

Noted (Tier 3, mouse; full text read, journal pre-proof): in a Pkhd1-dependent mouse model of immune-driven cholangitis, a T cell–cholangiocyte signal (CD226–NECTIN2) was found by spatial and single-cell mapping 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

Jördens MS, Nordhus KS, Øgaard J, et al., Melum E, Chung BK, J Hepatol 2026 (accepted Sept 16; DOI 10.1016/j.jhep.2026.09.021). NOD.c3c4 mice develop immune cells around bile ducts and then biliary cysts with limited fibrosis; T cells are required, and the authors state that a specific Pkhd1 mutation is necessary for the phenotype (Huang et al., J Immunol 2018, abstract read: a long terminal repeat inserted in intron 35 produces a truncated Pkhd1 transcript; disease needs the NOD genetic background, since the same mutation on C57BL/6 gave no clinical biliary disease, and needs the mutant Pkhd1 in both cholangiocytes and bone marrow). Spatial transcriptomics and single-cell sequencing placed immune cells almost entirely around bile ducts and pointed to CD226 on T cells and NECTIN2 on cholangiocytes, a pair whose gene is a known risk locus for primary sclerosing and biliary cholangitis. Lowering NECTIN2 on cultured cholangiocytes reduced T-cell activation, and an anti-CD226 antibody given for 5 weeks shifted liver T cells toward a less activated state without changing their number; in 10 human PSC livers, CD226+ T cells around NECTIN2+ cholangiocytes were more frequent than in 4 cirrhotic controls. Why it is noted: a Pkhd1-linked biliary cystic model in which immune activity is druggable, and an open map of T-cell interactions with cholangiocytes (Open Question 11). ⚠️ Limits: male mice and groups as small as n=2; the antibody study measured immune phenotype only, at 10 weeks, with no cyst, enzyme or fibrosis endpoints; autoimmune cholangitis on a NOD background is not ARPKD; human data are in PSC.

Noted (Tier 3; abstract and news release read): a UK team identifies "fibrosis-associated" liver blood vessel cells that appear early in fatty liver disease — adult MASLD, no ARPKD data 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

Gkantsinikoudi C, Dignam JP, et al., Fallowfield JA, Piper Hanley K, Alazawi W, Dufton NP, JCI Insight, in-press preview Sept 1, 2026 and October issue cover (DOI 10.1172/jci.insight.207327, open access; Queen Mary University of London with Manchester and Edinburgh; Queen Mary news release Oct 8, 2026). Using imaging, protein and gene-expression profiling and full-spectrum flow cytometry in mouse fibrosis models and samples from patients with metabolic fatty liver disease, the authors describe two subpopulations of endothelial cells that arise through endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (THY1.2+ICAM1+ and TAGLN+MCAM+). They call the TAGLN+ cells a conserved, pro-fibrotic type that appears early, before severe fibrosis, and report that higher liver TAGLN was linked to worse outcomes at every disease stage. The team is now investigating why these cells drive fibrosis and whether they can be targeted. Why it is noted: an early vascular marker and possible target for fibrosis, and a parallel to the idea in this issue that epithelial failure can lead to vascular failure and scarring. ⚠️ Limits: adult fatty liver models and patients; no ARPKD, biliary or pediatric data; full text not yet read.

Noted (Tier 2, peer reviewed; read through a news summary only): a 649,295-cell human liver atlas splits scar-associated macrophages in two — OLR1+ "SAMac2" cells expand in every chronic liver disease and predict worse outcomes 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

Papachristoforou E, Kong K, Colella F, et al., Fallowfield JA, Ramachandran P, Nature Genetics 58(10):2550–2565, 2026 (DOI 10.1038/s41588-026-02774-w, open access; University of Edinburgh). The atlas merges seven studies into 649,295 cells from 77 donors (42 healthy, 35 with chronic liver disease). Two scar-associated macrophage subsets, both TREM2+, differ sharply: SAMac1 carries reparative genes (GPNMB, MMP9, IL10, GDF15) and expands most in primary biliary cholangitis and primary sclerosing cholangitis; SAMac2 carries pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic genes (IL1B, TNF, NLRP3, PDGFB, OLR1) and expands in every cause. Spatial profiling put OLR1+ macrophages in fibrotic niches; in 632 staged fatty liver biopsies, high OLR1 or SAMac2 abundance was linked to higher mortality and faster decompensation; the same two subsets appeared in three mouse fibrosis models, including bile duct ligation, and arose from circulating monocytes. Knocking down OLR1 or using the clinical-stage antibody MEDI6570 lowered collagen expression in stellate-cell and spheroid models. Why it is noted: it adds a druggable macrophage target to the framework's macrophage thread, where fibrocystin-defective bile duct cells are known to recruit macrophages that promote portal fibrosis. ⚠️ Limits: adult disease and association-level human data; no ARPKD or congenital hepatic fibrosis samples; whether these cells exist in children with CHF is unknown (Open Question 8); we read a news summary, not the paper.

🦠 Gut-Liver Axis & Biliary Microbiome

🦠 GUT-LIVER AXIS & BILIARY MICROBIOME

Noted (Tier 3): gut-restricted FXR agonists cut appetite in obese mice through secretin and need leptin to work (full text read), with two related bile acid items seen as abstracts only 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

(1) Zhu X, Wang K, Hu H, Guo Y, … Xu X, Hao H, Wang H, EMBO Rep 2026 (DOI 10.1038/s44319-026-00938-7, open access, published Oct 5, 2026; full text read). Two intestine-restricted FXR agonists (fexaramine and Fex-3, 10 mg/kg by mouth) reduced body weight, fat mass and food intake in diet-induced obese and db/db mice, but not in leptin-deficient ob/ob mice unless low-dose leptin was added. The effect ran through secretin: serum secretin, which obesity had suppressed, was restored to roughly the level of chow-fed mice, and knocking secretin down removed the benefit. FGF15, the usual gut FXR messenger, rose only briefly and blocking its receptor did not blunt the effect. FXR activation raised an activating histone mark at the secretin gene, while leptin reduced DNA methylation there. Connection: it shows FXR can be switched on in the gut with effects far from the bile ducts, which bears on the sustained-versus-pulsatile FXR question; the authors cite the 2026 pulsatile FXR agonist paper (Zang et al., Nature) as part of why FXR drug development has been difficult, and cite a same-group 2026 paper (Song et al., Eur J Pharmacol 1025:178910, DOI 10.1016/j.ejphar.2026.178910; abstract and introduction read, methods and results not) reporting that Fex-3 relieved cholestatic liver injury in mice, depending on intestinal FXR and linked to suppressed hepatic CYP7A1, with good tolerability; its abstract frames the work around primary biliary cholangitis. Its introduction recalls that the systemic FXR agonist obeticholic acid was linked to serious liver injury in patients with cirrhosis and to intolerable itching, which is the authors' case for gut-restricted agonists. Older mouse work (the dual FXR/TGR5 agonist INT-767 and cilofexor in Mdr2-knockout mice, abstracts only) likewise found less biliary injury, and for cilofexor less fibrosis, through reduced bile acid synthesis. From general knowledge, secretin also drives cAMP-dependent secretion in cholangiocytes; here it was restored to normal in obese mice and not pushed above it, so the size of any biliary effect is unknown (our inference, untested). ⚠️ Limits: male mice, six per group, obesity models only, no cholestatic or cystic biliary endpoint.

(2) Xu et al., 2026 (abstract only): lithocholic acid improved glucose handling in diabetic mice through gut-microbe-derived secondary bile acids acting on TGR5 and GLP-1, with FXR suppression. Connection: TGR5 is one of the two cholangiocyte bile acid receptors we track; lithocholic acid is a known bile duct toxin in rodent cholestasis models (general knowledge), so it is not a lead. (3) Wang et al., J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle 2026 (abstract only): a 5:2 fasting pattern improved muscle impairment in mice on a high-fat diet; it restates the loop in which intestinal FXR drives FGF15, which acts on hepatic FGFR4 to suppress CYP7A1. No dietary or fasting inference follows for children.

🧪 Gene Therapy & Base Editing

🧪 GENE THERAPY & BASE EDITING

⭐⭐ Human-liver AAV capsid screening on machine perfusion: the standard clinical capsids reach hepatocytes well and cholangiocytes poorly — Nature Biotechnology (published January 2025; added to our tracking October 2, 2026) 📌 NEW · Oct 2, 2026

Kim JJ, Kurial SNT, et al. (senior author H. Willenbring, UCSF). "AAV capsid prioritization in normal and steatotic human livers maintained by machine perfusion." Nat Biotechnol 43(12):1966–1978, 2025. DOI: 10.1038/s41587-024-02523-6. Open access (CC BY). ✅ Full text read October 2, 2026. Tier 2.

Method: Barcoded AAV vectors were infused through the portal vein of discarded adult human livers kept alive on normothermic machine perfusion, and the transduced cells were profiled by single-cell RNA sequencing. AAV8 was studied in two normal livers; AAV8, AAV5, AAV-LK03, AAV6 and AAV-NP59 were compared in four livers (two normal, two steatotic).

Findings: All five capsids transduced mainly hepatocytes, and cholangiocytes showed low transduction. AAV-LK03 was the most hepatocyte-specific. AAV8 also reached Kupffer cells (up to 8.6%) and sinusoidal endothelial cells. In fatty livers the vector formed fewer concatemers, so strategies that depend on DNA recombination — dual-vector delivery of large genes and Cas9 with AAV repair templates — may work less well there.

Why it matters for ARPKD-CHF: this is the first single-cell evidence in intact human liver that the standard clinical capsids reach cholangiocytes poorly. Biliary delivery therefore needs new tools: capsids engineered or screened for cholangiocytes, or a different route. The authors themselves propose using this platform to screen capsid libraries for delivery to cholangiocytes in inherited biliary disease. The paper also notes fatal liver failure after high-dose AAV8 in X-linked myotubular myopathy.

⚠️ Limits: discarded adult livers, two to four per comparison, portal-vein delivery only. No cholangiopathic liver was studied and retrograde delivery through the bile duct was not tested. Low cholangiocyte transduction in normal human liver does not show how cyst-lining biliary epithelium would behave.

Noted (Tier 3 context): meta-analysis of immunosuppression in clinical AAV gene therapy — 22 liver-directed trials show frequent antibody responses and liver enzyme rises despite steroids; no regimen shown superior 📌 NOTED · Oct 5, 2026

Wang Z, Wu N, Fu D, Zhao Q, Wang H. "A systematic review and meta-analysis of immunosuppressive regimens and immune-related outcomes in adeno-associated virus-based gene therapy." Sci Rep 2026 (Article in Press; published online October 1, 2026). DOI: 10.1038/s41598-026-71872-w. Open access (CC BY 4.0). ✅ Full text read October 5, 2026.

73 studies and 1,773 participants. The 22 liver-directed studies (455 participants, all intravenous, mostly hemophilia) pooled to a 75.8% humoral immune response, 49.5% elevated ALT and 26.0% cellular response. Corticosteroids were used in 71 of the 73 studies, and the authors found no regimen shown to be superior; certainty was very low for most outcomes and most studies were single-arm. Why it is noted here: a benchmark for what systemic AAV does even in adults with normal bile ducts. ⚠️ Limits: no biliary disease, no children's liver disease and no gene editing were examined, so applicability to ARPKD-CHF is untested.

Noted (Tier 5, context): PKD Foundation research webinar — PKD1 is too large for AAV, so the field is testing protein fragments and base editors 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

Webinar transcript with Dr. Michael Caplan (Yale) and Dr. Xiaogang Li (Mayo); no peer-reviewed source. Published or stated points only: the PKD1 coding sequence is about 13 kb against an AAV capacity near 4.5 kb. Caplan's lab expresses only the 200-amino-acid polycystin-1 C-terminal tail (about 600 bp, fits in AAV); the benefit was seen only in mice that carry the mitochondrial enzyme NNT. Li reported kidney base-editing efficiency of about 10–15% with a universal promoter and 20–25% with a kidney-specific one in mice, durable for 6 months, with under 1% bystander editing. Both stressed that all results are in mice and that early treatment matters. Why it is noted: a precedent for fibrocystin-fragment delivery and a benchmark for editing efficiency. Unpublished results mentioned in the webinar are intentionally not recorded here.

Also seen (headline-level, abstracts not read): gene therapy in other diseases — a mechanism-based strategy review, a vascular gene therapy, a recessive-disease precedent, and the monitoring and readiness side of AAV treatment 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

Listed with the connection I could find for each; none involves ARPKD. (1) Zhang et al., Mol Ther Nucleic Acids 2026, a review of gene therapy for hereditary neuromuscular diseases, organizes strategies by mechanism: loss-of-function disorders by gene replacement, gene editing or RNA-based correction, and gain-of-function disorders by silencing or removing the toxic product. Connection: ARPKD is loss of function, so the same three options apply; from general knowledge (not the snippet), the muscular dystrophy field also supplies the best-known precedent for delivering a shortened working version of a gene too large for AAV, the logic behind the polycystin-1 and fibrocystin fragment ideas. I could not locate the Zhang review itself; a 2026 review in Front Cell Dev Biol (DOI 10.3389/fcell.2026.1765367) states the same framework and notes that AAV gene replacement suits small genes with recessive loss of function (SMN1, SGCA, FKRP), which PKHD1 is not. (2) Bogdan et al., 2026: a VEGF165/Ang-1 plasmid increased capillary density in rat limb ischemia over 28 days. Connection: proof that gene delivery can rebuild vessels, relevant to the framework's idea that epithelial failure leads to vascular failure and scarring; the caution is that new vessels also feed portal hypertension, so the direction of any vascular approach in the liver is unresolved. (3) "Night Blindness–Retinitis Pigmentosa" reference entry: voretigene neparvovec is approved for biallelic RPE65 disease, and in its phase 3 trial 65% of treated patients completed a low-light mobility course. Connection: an approved gene replacement for a biallelic recessive disease, delivered locally to a small organ with a small gene, the opposite of PKHD1's size and access problem. (4) Wang et al., Clin Chem 2026 (B-216): validating a total antibody test for AAV9. Connection: pre-existing anti-AAV antibodies reduce delivery and add safety risk, and screening assays are a prerequisite for any AAV trial; this one is for AAV9, not the capsids discussed here. (5) Sobolak et al., Clin Chem 2026: troponin assays for cardiac monitoring during SMA and Duchenne gene therapy, which the FDA recommends. Connection: shows what an AAV safety-monitoring panel includes beyond liver enzymes. (6) Garcia Uzquiano et al., Front Med: 67.6% of pediatric neurologists were strongly willing to adopt Duchenne gene therapy, with clinical-readiness barriers. Connection: a template for asking pediatric hepatologists and nephrologists the same question about ARPKD. (7) Tattan, analytical strategy for gene therapies, and Rodriguez Lopez et al., 2026 (carbon footprint of hemophilia B gene therapy versus lifelong prophylaxis): development-analytics and one-time-versus-lifelong assessment frameworks that any future ARPKD program would need. Two other items in the alert had no connection I could find.

Noted (Tier 3, 2020 editorial; added to our tracking this month): two children died of liver toxicity after high-dose AAV for X-linked myotubular myopathy — the dose was three times the one that had worked 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

Wilson JM and Flotte TR, Hum Gene Ther 31(13–14):695–696, 2020 (DOI 10.1089/hum.2020.182; text read, matched by content). Six children given 1 × 1014 genome copies/kg did well; all three given 3 × 1014 developed severe liver toxicity and two died, and development was put on hold. The authors place this beside milder platelet, liver and complement-related kidney effects in Duchenne trials at similar doses, and severe liver and clotting toxicity in non-human primates at 2 × 1014. Proposed explanations include antibody-vector complexes that activate complement; they list age, weight-based dosing, even low-titer pre-formed antibodies, genetic predisposition, capsid, promoter and purification as variables to study. Why it is noted: a published, citable account of what high systemic AAV doses can do, relevant to any AAV plan for children whose platelets and kidneys are already under strain. ⚠️ Limits: an editorial written early; it does not mention pre-existing liver or bile duct disease, so it does not settle the cholangiopathy question.

Noted (Tier 4, headline-level, abstract not read): bispecific antibodies that engage the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR) enrich in mucosal tissue in monkeys — its introduction names ARPKD among epithelial diseases hard to reach with circulating antibodies 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

Marks K, Shah N, Pham L, Chen J, White I, Burke K, et al., mAbs 2026 (PMID 42829333; online Oct 3, 2026; seen only as a search snippet, so the data and the design are not known to us). pIgR carries polymeric IgA and IgM across epithelial cells from the blood side to the lumen; ordinary IgG has no such route, and earlier work attached pIgR-binding fragments to antibodies so that they cross epithelia (mAbs 2020 and a 2026 Front Immunol report, both in lung and gut models). The snippet's introduction says that in polycystic kidney disease, including ADPKD and ARPKD, the overgrown tubule epithelium is inaccessible to circulating therapeutic antibodies. Why it is noted: it bears on Open Question 4 (how to reach epithelial cells). From general knowledge, cholangiocytes also express pIgR and move IgA into bile, so an antibody shuttle could in principle reach the bile duct lumen; this is our inference and nothing here tests it. Transport runs toward the lumen, so cargo would reach cyst or duct fluid and the cilium, not the inside of the cell, and it would need a target accessible from the lumen. ⚠️ Limits: abstract-level; the paper's tissue data (kidney, liver) are unknown to us.

🏥 Clinical & Biomarkers

🏥 CLINICAL & BIOMARKERS

⭐⭐ PKHD1 missense variant at last exon nucleotide (-1G position) causes complete exon skipping in Caroli disease — UREC urine RNA testing method validated for PKHD1 variant reclassification — Gene (August 4, 2026 online / November 20, 2026 print) 📌 NEW · Oct 1, 2026

Huang H, Cheng C, Chen P, Yang F, Pan S, Xie F, Zhao S, Chen X (corresponding). "A novel PKHD1 missense variant disrupting splicing in a fetus with Caroli disease." Gene 1011:150340, November 20, 2026. Published online August 4, 2026. DOI: 10.1016/j.gene.2026.150340. PMID: 42551719. ⚠️ NOT open access — Elsevier all rights reserved. ✅ Abstract and highlights confirmed October 1, 2026. Case Report.

Case: Fetus at 23 weeks gestation. Hepatic cysts on ultrasound, "central dot" sign on MRI (Caroli disease), ARPKD features, oligohydramnios. Exome sequencing: paternal nonsense c.5323C>T (Arg1775*); two maternal missense variants c.6682G>C (Glu2228Gln) and c.8012G>T (Arg2671Leu).

Key finding — c.6682G>C is the last nucleotide of exon 40 (-1G position): Minigene assay confirmed complete exon 40 skipping → in-frame deletion of 64 amino acids (Gly2164_Arg2227del) within a β-barrel domain of fibrocystin. AlphaFold3 predicts this deletion compromises β-barrel structural stability. The variant was initially annotated as missense (Glu2228Gln) but its primary effect is splice site disruption — not amino acid substitution.

Four highlights with direct ARPKD-CHF implications:

(1) Exonic last-nucleotide (-1G) variants cause severe splice disruption and exon skipping. This validates a variant class that is likely underdiagnosed across PKHD1 variant databases — many variants annotated as missense at exon-intron boundaries may be primarily splice disruptors.

(2) Systematic retrospective re-evaluation of unclassified PKHD1 VUS using splice-aware tools is needed. Any PKHD1 VUS with "predicted splice impact" annotation should be re-evaluated with SpliceAI and, where possible, functional RNA analysis.

(3) Endogenous RNA splicing in patient-derived primary cells (URECs/organoids) reveals tissue-specific penetrance. URECs (urine-derived renal epithelial cells) can be collected non-invasively from a urine sample and used to test whether a specific PKHD1 variant causes exon skipping in the patient's own cells. Tissue-specific penetrance means splicing may differ between kidney and liver cells — directly relevant to liver-dominant ARPKD-CHF.

(4) Structural and transcriptomic integration refines genotype-phenotype spectrum in ARPKD/Caroli disease.

⚠️ What this paper does NOT establish for our daughter's variant c.10926G>A (Met3642Ile): We confirmed October 1, 2026 that c.10926G>A is at genomic position chr6:51,659,200 (hg38) — confirmed from ClinVar primary database. This position is 268 bp interior to the exon that also contains c.10658 (Ile3553Thr, chr6:51,659,468). Because the genomic distance (268 bp) equals the CDS distance exactly, there are no introns between the two variants — they are in the same exon. Therefore c.10926G>A is NOT a -1G last-nucleotide splice variant of the class studied in Huang et al. It is located within the interior of its exon, not at the splice donor boundary. Its splice disruption (if real) would operate through ESE motif disruption — a different and less predictable mechanism. ClinVar: Conflicting classifications — all three reporting laboratories classify as VUS (uncertain significance). No published functional RNA data for c.10926G>A exists.

What the Huang paper still opens for c.10926G>A: Highlight 3 — UREC RNA testing — applies regardless of mechanism class. A urine sample from our daughter could directly test whether c.10926G>A causes exon skipping in her own cells, via endogenous RNA analysis without biopsy, iPSC reprogramming, or organoid culture. This is the most direct available experiment. Highlight 2 — systematic VUS re-evaluation — also applies: c.10926G>A was run through SpliceAI and Pangolin on Oct 8, 2026 (hg38 chr6:51,659,200 C>T): every delta score was 0.05 or lower, so these tools predict no splice effect, and they are known to miss enhancer-mediated skipping, so only an RNA or minigene test can settle it.

Companion paper: PMID 42697289 ("Structural and diagnostic implications of exonic splice-disrupting variants in PKHD1-related Caroli disease") refers to the Huang et al. case report — likely a review or commentary. Not yet indexed. New contact: Xinlin Chen (corresponding author of Huang et al.) — email not yet identified. Priority contact for UREC testing methodology.

Noted (Tier 4, conference abstract — not peer reviewed): two-year follow-up of liver stiffness by transient elastography in children with cystic kidney disease — 7 of 9 ARPKD children had raised stiffness and 3 progressed 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

Jacobs A, Kapil S, Morlan W, Gupta S, Molleston J (Indiana University / Riley Hospital for Children). Abstract 80, JPGN Reports 7(S2):S55, 2026 (supplement DOI 10.1002/jpr3.70233). 28 children with cystic kidney disease (9 ARPKD); FibroScan at enrollment and 2 years. Seven of nine ARPKD children had stiffness more than 2 SD above the mean (7.3 to 32.7 kPa); three progressed (32.7→59.8, 18.3→46.8, 13.1→17.8 kPa), two with worsening platelet counts. One nephronophthisis child with very high stiffness died of a ruptured splenic artery pseudoaneurysm. ⚠️ Limits: nine ARPKD patients at one center identified by billing codes; the reference for "more than 2 SD above the mean" is not stated; do not compare these kPa values with MR elastography values. Noted (Tier 3): first-in-human dosimetry of a PET tracer for active fibrosis (PDGFRβ) — not yet tested in biliary disease or children 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

Noted (Tier 2, published 2010; added to our tracking this month): in 74 fetuses and early-demise neonates with ARPKD, two truncating PKHD1 variants predicted kidney severity but not liver fibrosis 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

Noted (Tier 3, review; full text read): a review of cholangiocyte plasticity and cell therapy outlines organoid transplantation into bile ducts, but has no ARPKD data 📌 NOTED · Oct 2026

GENETICS & VARIANTS
Q1. Which PKHD1 variants cause liver-predominant disease? Cohorts so far (Burgmaier's zones, Zhao, Lapunzina-Soler, and Bhuva's nine congenital hepatic fibrosis cases) have not been pooled into one table of variant, position and phenotype. Do missense variants in the C-terminal ectodomain (Zone 3, AA 2625–4074) cluster in liver-predominant cases? A 2025 series of prenatally diagnosed Caroli-type cases found three of four missense variants in this region, but the numbers are tiny. An older French cohort (Denamur 2010) lists missense variants in the same region (W2736G, I2957T, R3240K) among children who died perinatally, so the region is not liver-only. Small case reports point both ways (Giacobbe 2022; and a prenatally diagnosed Caroli case with one missense variant in this region who needed kidney and liver transplants by 16 months, Rivas 2019).
Q2. Does an interior-exon missense variant of uncertain significance, such as c.10926G>A (p.Met3642Ile), change splicing? Precedents exist (R760H through an enhancer motif; a last-nucleotide missense with exon 40 skipping). Would a minigene assay or urine-cell RNA testing settle it? (A SpliceAI and Pangolin lookup on Oct 8 predicted no splice effect, with all scores 0.05 or lower; these tools miss enhancer-mediated skipping.)
Q3. Do modifier genes beyond PKHD1 explain the wide variation in ARPKD severity, including liver involvement? Does the UK PKD genetics fellowship's dataset include ARPKD patients? Candidates raised in the older literature, from animal work, include HNF1B, KIF12 and interactions with PKD1 and PKD2; human evidence is lacking. A 2018 mouse study found that a truncating Pkhd1 variant caused clinical biliary disease only on the NOD autoimmune background (Huang et al.).
DELIVERY & THERAPY
Q4. How can cholangiocytes be reached? Five clinical AAV capsids transduced them poorly in perfused human livers. Has anyone screened capsids or tried retrograde bile-duct delivery for cholangiocytes? A 2026 antibody report (pIgR-engaging bispecifics, abstract level) names ARPKD in its introduction as a disease hard to reach with circulating antibodies.
Q5. Is there a primary, citable report of AAV-associated liver failure in children with pre-existing cholangiopathy, and does it apply to congenital hepatic fibrosis? The 2020 editorial on the myotubular myopathy deaths describes liver toxicity at high dose but does not mention pre-existing cholangiopathy.
Q6. Would activating long-form PDE4 (LoAc® compounds) reduce cyst growth in ARPKD cholangiocytes without worsening stellate-cell fibrosis? Published PDE4D data point the other way for fibrosis.
BIOMARKERS & TRIALS
Q7. What liver endpoint could regulators accept for ARPKD-CHF: a stiffness trend, platelet count and spleen size, a blood marker, or an imaging tracer? What are the pediatric reference ranges, and how should values from different elastography methods be compared?
Q8. Is PKHD1 detectable in healthy children's cholangiocytes in the pediatric liver atlas, and do adult-derived findings (for example the OLR1+ macrophages) hold at a pediatric baseline?
MODELS & CELLS
Q9. Can urine-derived cells from children with ARPKD-CHF yield cholangiocyte or assembloid models, and which labs are set up to receive samples with proper consent?
BILE ACID SIGNALING
Q10. Would a gut-restricted FXR agonist, which lowers bile acid synthesis through FGF15 without exposing the bile ducts, help ARPKD biliary disease? And would the accompanying rise in secretin, a cAMP-raising signal in cholangiocytes, matter in cystic models? A same-group 2026 report of Fex-3 in cholestatic liver injury (framed around primary biliary cholangitis) exists; only its abstract and introduction have been read here.
IMMUNE–CHOLANGIOCYTE INTERACTIONS
Q11. Do PKHD1-deficient human cholangiocytes and ARPKD-CHF livers show immune interactions like the T cell–cholangiocyte CD226–NECTIN2 signal found in the Pkhd1-dependent NOD.c3c4 mouse? Is peribiliary T-cell infiltration part of ARPKD-CHF, and could immune-modulating antibodies be tested in that model?
CELL THERAPY
Q12. Could patient-derived, gene-corrected cholangiocyte organoids be delivered into the bile ducts of a Pkhd1-deficient model, and would the corrected cells engraft and persist in cystic, fibrotic ducts as they do in healthy or acutely injured ones?

7th SIGN conference — March 19–21, 2027, San Sebastián, Spain

The Society for Image-Guided Neurointerventions (SIGN), founded in 2018, will hold its 7th conference at Palacio Miramar in Donostia–San Sebastián, in collaboration with CIC biomaGUNE. Its leadership includes University of Maryland investigators Dr. Piotr Walczak and Professor Miroslaw Janowski, whose hepatic-artery delivery work appeared in Issue 3.

The society’s focus is image-guided drug delivery to the brain and spinal cord rather than liver or kidney disease. It is included here at the organizers’ request and is not an endorsement.
